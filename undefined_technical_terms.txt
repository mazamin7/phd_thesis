[R2-01] "global support", "computational domain", "interpolant"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Location:
  Chapter 1, Section 1.3 ("Finite Element and Spectral Methods")
  Printed Page: P9 | TeX Source: latex/Thesis.tex, Line 385
- Excerpt from TeX:
  "In spectral methods, by contrast, the basis functions have global support over
  the computational domain, so that the solution is represented by a single global
  interpolant and the spatial derivatives are obtained by differentiating this
  interpolant."
- Reviewer 2 Comment:
  "the terms 'global support', 'computational domain' and 'interpolant' have not
  been defined in this context."
- Deficit Analysis:
  * "computational domain": Used as undefined jargon. In PDE boundary value problems,
    it denotes the bounded open spatial region \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d on which
    the governing equation is solved subject to conditions on its boundary \partial\Omega.
  * "global support": Contrasted with local support in FEM, but "support"
    supp(\phi_k) = closure(\{x \in \Omega : \phi_k(x) \neq 0\}) is never
    defined. Basis functions in spectral methods are non-zero almost everywhere across
    the entire domain \Omega, rather than vanishing outside a compact sub-element.
  * "interpolant": The function I_N u(x) = \sum_{k=0}^N c_k \phi_k(x)
    satisfying I_N u(x_j) = u(x_j) at grid collocation points x_j is
    not mathematically specified.
- Required Fix (Reviewer 2 -> RED):
  Provide concise definitions and references (Hesthaven et al., 2007; Canuto et al., 2006):
  1. Define the computational domain \Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d with boundary \partial\Omega.
  2. Define support: basis functions have global support because supp(\phi_k) = closure(\Omega),
     meaning each basis function extends across the entire spatial domain without vanishing outside local elements.
  3. Define interpolant: a global function I_N u(x) formed as a linear expansion in the basis
     functions that matches the nodal values of the field at discrete grid nodes x_j, whose derivative
     d/dx I_N u(x) supplies the discrete differential operator.

[R2-02] "spectral convergence", "exponential convergence", "numerical dispersion"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Location:
  Chapter 1, Section 1.3 ("Finite Element and Spectral Methods")
  Printed Page: P9 | TeX Source: latex/Thesis.tex, Line 387
  (See also Line 378 for numerical dispersion, and Lines 3680, 3725, 3794 for exponential convergence)
- Excerpt from TeX:
  "Because of their global approximation, Fourier spectral methods, namely
  spectral methods based on trigonometric interpolants, can be interpreted as
  infinite-order finite-difference schemes on uniform grids. For sufficiently
  smooth solutions, they exhibit spectral, often exponential, convergence and
  very low numerical dispersion (\cite{hesthaven_spectral_2007})."
- Reviewer 2 Comment:
  "neither spectral convergence, exponential convergence or numerical dispersion
  have been defined."
- Deficit Analysis:
  * "spectral convergence": Also called infinite-order convergence; the error decays
    faster than any algebraic power N^{-p} of the number of degrees of freedom N
    for infinitely smooth (C^\infty) solutions (||u - I_N u|| = O(N^{-k})
    for every k > 0).
  * "exponential convergence": When the solution is analytic in a strip around the real
    axis, the error decays geometrically/exponentially as O(e^{-c N}) for some
    constant c > 0. The text treats "spectral" and "exponential" interchangeably without
    clarifying the distinction.
  * "numerical dispersion": Phase velocity error introduced by spatial and temporal
    discretization, causing different harmonic frequency components to propagate at
    unphysical numerical phase speeds c_num(\omega) \neq c, resulting in artificial
    pulse deformation and phase error.
- Required Fix (Reviewer 2 -> RED):
  Formally define:
  1. Spectral/infinite-order convergence: error decays faster than O(N^{-p})
     for any algebraic power p for smooth functions;
  2. Exponential (geometric) convergence: error bounds of form ||u - u_N|| \le C e^{-\eta N}
     for analytic functions;
  3. Numerical dispersion: the unphysical dependence of the numerical wave speed on grid spacing
     and frequency (\omega_{num}(k) \neq c k), citing Trefethen (2000) and Hesthaven et al. (2007).

[R2-04] "transmission conditions"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Location:
  Chapter 1, Section 1.4 ("Domain Decomposition Methods")
  Printed Page: P11 | TeX Source: latex/Thesis.tex, Lines 406, 408
  (Also mentioned on P13 Line 431, P14 Line 440, and P71 Line 3842)
- Excerpt from TeX:
  "A related approach is the Optimized Schwarz method, where suitable transmission
  conditions are designed to improve the convergence rate of the iteration."

- Reviewer 2 Comment:
  "P11: 'transmission conditions' have not been defined in this context"

  boundary traces and normal fluxes during iterative Schwarz cycles (citing Quarteroni & Valli,
  1999; Gander, 2006).

[R2-05] "the amplification polynomial"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Location:
  Chapter 2, Section 2.4.4 ("Analysis - Von Neumann Stability")
  Printed Page: P38 | TeX Source: latex/Thesis.tex, Line 1857
- Excerpt from TeX:
  "Introducing the dimensionless parameters \sigma = \frac{c\Delta t}{\Delta x},
  \alpha = \frac{\gamma\Delta t}{2}, \beta = \frac{\nu\Delta t}{2\Delta x^2},
  we obtain the amplification polynomial
  (1+\alpha+2\beta\delta)\xi^2 - 2(1-\sigma^2\delta)\xi + (1-\alpha-2\beta\delta) = 0,
  which can be rewritten as a\xi^2+b\xi+c=0..."
- Reviewer 2 Comment:
  "P38: 'the amplification polynomial': why is it called like this?"
- Deficit Analysis:
  The author introduces \xi as the "amplification factor" (u_j^{n+1} = \xi u_j^n), but
  does not explain why setting the resulting characteristic expression to zero yields the
  "amplification polynomial". It is the characteristic polynomial of the linear discrete
  time-evolution operator whose roots \xi define the growth/decay factor of each spatial
  Fourier mode per discrete time step.
- Required Fix (Reviewer 2 -> RED):
  Clarify why it is called the amplification polynomial: Explain that in von Neumann stability
  analysis, the discrete evolution is expressed as u^{n+1} = \xi u^n. Inserting this harmonic
  ansatz into the multi-level difference scheme yields a characteristic polynomial in \xi,
  P(\xi) = a\xi^2 + b\xi + c = 0. The roots of this algebraic equation are the amplification
  factors \xi(k) that govern the modal growth per time step. Hence, P(\xi) is the
  amplification (or characteristic) polynomial of the scheme (cite Strikwerda, 2004; Hirsch, 2007).

[R2-06] "explicit leapfrog FDTD" and "first-order Velocity Verlet scheme"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Location:
  Chapter 3, Section 3.2.3 ("Application to FDTD")
  Printed Page: P87 | TeX Source: latex/Thesis.tex, Line 4413
- Excerpt from TeX:
  "For the purely undamped wave equation, standard explicit leapfrog FDTD is
  mathematically equivalent to a first-order Velocity Verlet scheme, making the
  Strang-split ARD integration straightforward."
- Reviewer 2 Comment:
  "P87: 'explicit leapfrog FDTD' and 'first-order Velocity Verlet scheme' are not defined"
- Deficit Analysis:
  * "explicit leapfrog FDTD": Standard staggered-in-time discretization where position/pressure
    is evaluated at integer time steps t^n and velocity at half-integer time steps t^{n+1/2},
    leaping over one another.
  * "first-order Velocity Verlet scheme": Velocity Verlet is a symplectic second-order
    integrator from Hamiltonian dynamics / molecular dynamics that splits the update into
    position and velocity drift/kick steps. Calling it "first-order" is both confusing
    (Velocity Verlet is 2nd-order accurate in time) and undefined without writing down the equations
    or demonstrating the equivalence.
- Required Fix (Reviewer 2 -> RED):
  1. Define explicit leapfrog FDTD: write the staggered time-stepping equations
     v^{n+1/2} = v^{n-1/2} + \Delta t L_h u^n, u^{n+1} = u^n + \Delta t v^{n+1/2}.
  2. Define the Velocity Verlet scheme: write the Kick-Drift-Kick update
     v^{n+1/2} = v^n + (\Delta t/2) a(u^n), u^{n+1} = u^n + \Delta t v^{n+1/2},
     v^{n+1} = v^{n+1/2} + (\Delta t/2) a(u^{n+1}).
  3. Prove or show their mathematical equivalence for undamped linear wave systems (a(u) = c^2 \Delta_h u),
     citing Hairer et al. (2006) and Swope et al. (1982), and clarify the confusing
     "first-order" label (it represents a first-order system in time, i.e., state-space formulation, with
     second-order temporal accuracy).

- Deficit: FDTD is an algebraic, second-order finite-difference scheme (O(\Delta x^2)).
  Calling its convergence "exponential" in the text contradicts Table 2.1 (which reports
  algebraic convergence rates around 1.0 to 3.2). At CFL=1 in 1D, the magic time-step
  exhibits exact numerical propagation (error drops to machine precision), not
  "exponential convergence".
- Fix: Correct the text to state that at CFL=1 the 1D scheme achieves exact cancellation
  (zero numerical dispersion) and second-order algebraic convergence generally, removing
  the incorrect phrase "exponential convergence" for FDTD.
